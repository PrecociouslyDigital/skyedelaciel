# Design Goals

Content on this site should be optimized for **reading prose on desktop**. Where possible, pages should **degrade gracefully** on other form factors (such as supporting vertical displays or touchscreens), support screen-readers with aria tags where appropriate, and **print well** directly from the browser. Elements should be padded pleasantly, both from each other and the edge of the screen.

Inspirations include Edward Tufte and Gwern.net.

The visual vocabulary is borrowed from Chinese calligraphy and the page furniture of a woodblock book. Paper and ink give the two screen schemes their names and a vermilion seal gives them their one accent. A frame of a thick rule outside a thin one holds the text block. Notes sit outside that frame, as marginal commentary does. Rules between sections are single tapered brush strokes rather than hairlines. No Chinese characters are used as ornament; only the shapes are.

A second hand has since read the page and marked it up. Surveillance interfaces share the grammar of scholarly apparatus — a box drawn around a subject, a readout in the margin, a timestamp that authenticates the record — and differ only in who drew them, so the machine's layer is added to the page rather than substituted for it. Where the scholar ruled a frame in ink, a machine sets corner ticks outside it in malachite; where the seal authenticates the document by human authority, a revision stamp down the right rail authenticates it by build. Both are visible at once, and neither is allowed to set running text. See [Annotation](#annotation).

# Article Defaults

Most content on this site is a single article on a single page.

## Front Matter

An article's front matter (not to be conflated with markdown's `yaml` frontmatter) contains human-relevant metadata about the article. It should begin every article, visually set apart, closed off by a brush rule in the accent colour. Where the article itself is not framed — a narrow viewport, or paper — the front matter wears the frame instead, so a reader always meets one ruled block before the text.

### Title

The first front matter to appear should be the title, which should be visually more important than anything else in the article.

### Author

The author should appear after the title, less visually notable than most text. It should appear on the same line if the viewport has enough horizontal space, or wrap to its own line otherwise.

### Abstract

The abstract should follow the title and author. It should be formatted similarly to article text, but still stand out at a glance.

## Content

The main content of the article should have a maximum width of 80 characters. Where there is room on either side of it, the article is framed: a thick rule outside a thin one, with a channel of paper between them, and the text held off that frame by about two characters.

### Block elements

A blockquote is set in from a rule of solid ink on its left. Bullets are marked in the accent colour and numbers in muted ink. Inline code sits on a faintly tinted ground; a code block sits on the raised surface inside a single hairline. A table rules its header row off with a stroke of ink and each body row with a hairline, and sets its column headers in the same small caps as the rest of the apparatus. A horizontal rule is a brush stroke, centred and well short of the measure.

## Colors

There should be three color schemes: paper (light), ink (dark) and print. Paper and ink should default to the system preference, based on the css media query `prefers-color-scheme`, but should also be toggleable by the user (see [Navbar](#navbar)). Once a user sets a theme preference, that preference should be persisted to local storage and reloaded as early as possible in page load. Print is a scheme of its own and follows no preference.

Every scheme names the same roles, and only those: a `background`, a `raised` surface for what sits on top of it, `text` and a quieter `muted` for apparatus and marginal notes, a `border` rule with a fainter `border-faint` for tinted ground, three chromas — `accent`, `signal` and `attention` — and a `shadow`. A scheme that omits a role, or invents one, should fail the build, as should one whose text falls below 7:1 against its own background or whose muted text or any of whose chromas falls below 4.5:1. Print is the boldest of the three schemes, and that too is a build error rather than a matter of taste.

The three chromas are three hands, and none of them ever sets running text.

| Role        | Whose hand it is                                    | Where it appears                                                                                            |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `accent`    | the author — stamped, rare, permanent               | the logo, the rule closing the front matter, sidenote numbers, bullets, the focus ring                      |
| `signal`    | the machine that read the page — ambient, constant  | corner ticks, the rail slug, link provenance marks                                                          |
| `attention` | right now — hot, transient, gone when you look away | the current entry in the table of contents, the underline under a hovered link, a link that did not resolve |

`accent` is the vermilion of a seal pressed onto a document; `signal` is malachite and `attention` ochre, which is the traditional pairing beside vermilion rather than an import from a screen. In the light scheme all three sit between 6 and 7:1 against the background, so no one of them shouts louder than the others by contrast alone.

## Annotation

The machine layer is one idea, and everything drawn in `signal` belongs to it: a second reader has been over this page and left its working visible. It never writes words of its own — it draws boxes and it stamps identifiers, which is the whole of its vocabulary.

It draws a **bounding box** as four corner ticks, a right angle at each corner with the edges between them left open, around anything it has taken as a subject: the article, outside the ink frame rather than replacing it, so that both readers' boxes stand; a code block; the popover on a link. Where the thing it has boxed can scroll, the ticks are painted on it rather than around it, since a box drawn outside a scrolling element would be clipped.

It draws a **rail slug** down the right margin of a framed article, set in a vertical column in the monospace face — the 書口 of a woodblock page, which carries the title and fascicle number down the fold. The column runs downward but the characters stand upright in it, as they do on a seal, rather than being turned on their side. It dates the page: published, edited if it has been, and the revision the build was made from. An unedited page carries no edited date, and outside a git checkout the revision is simply absent.

It draws a **provenance mark** after every link in the prose: a single glyph naming what kind of address it is — `§` for a page of this site, `↗` for anywhere else, `¶` for a DOI, `W` for Wikipedia. The mark is a property of the kind, so a kind without one should fail to compile. Whether the link resolved is a separate question and is answered by colour rather than by a second glyph: a link nothing answered for keeps its own mark, drawn in `attention`. A same-document fragment is not unresolved — there was nothing to look up.

The boxes and the marks are not printed: paper carries no live annotation, and a tick drawn on a sheet is a mark no reader can follow up. The rail slug is the exception and prints with the page, standing in the clear strip the text block leaves beside it. It is a dateline rather than an affordance, and a sheet separated from its address has nothing else to say when it was written or which revision it is.

## Typography

Prose is set in Source Serif 4, the Latin companion of Source Han Serif and so the text face that sits comfortably beside Song-style type. Display type — an article's title and every heading — is set in Cormorant, whose high contrast and drawn-out terminals read as brush work at display sizes. Apparatus — navigation links, the byline, column headers, the contents toggle — is the prose face set as letterspaced small caps, so that labels stay in the same voice as the text while reading as captions. Code is set in whatever monospace the reader already has.

Both families are variable, and both are self-hosted: every font file a page loads should come from this site's own origin, so that reading a page announces the reader to nobody else.

The scale runs from the article title, which is the largest thing on the page, down through the six heading levels to body text at 1.0625rem on a line height of 1.6. Figures in prose are old-style, so that a number sits in the line of text rather than standing above it; figures in tables and in ordered-list markers are lining and tabular, so that columns of them align.

## Motion

A readout snaps to where it belongs rather than easing into it. Every transition on the site is linear, runs no longer than 120ms, and moves only geometry or colour; a longer one should fail the build rather than be caught by eye. Delays are exempt from that limit and are a separate tool — the link popover waits before it appears, so that a pointer crossing a link on its way somewhere else never summons one.

A reader who has asked for reduced motion gets none of it: under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` every transition duration is zero. That answer should travel with each transition rather than being applied once over the top of everything, so a component that grows a new one cannot forget it.

# Components

## Navbar

### Wide viewports

When there is enough space for both the navbar and sidenotes, the navbar sits to the side of the content, right-aligned to its left edge, and is arranged vertically.

### Narrow viewports

The navbar is horizontal, in line with the content, before the title. It should match the content's maximum width so they align visually.

### Print

The Navbar should not be shown on print pages, and should make space so that the content is aligned to the left edge of the page.

### Content

The navbar contains, in order

- A clickable logo that takes you to the homepage
- Links to various internal and external landing pages
- A button that toggles the [Color Scheme](#colors)

The logo is drawn as a seal: the mark in accent-coloured line, inside a square border of the same colour. It is painted rather than served as an image, so that it reads in both schemes without a second copy of the artwork. The scheme toggle is a small square, matching the seal and the frame rather than the circle it used to be.

## Table of contents

### Wide viewports

When there is enough space for both the table of contents and sidenotes, the table of contents sits to the left of the content, arranged vertically, underneath the navbar. As the user scrolls, the top of the table of contents should "stick" to the top of the viewport (with pleasant margin, of course).

The table of contents should have each level be collapsible (so you'd expand a specific `h1` to see `h2`'s under it). Collapse should be controlled by an indicator to the _right_ of that element. A vertical line to the left of siblings groups them together. When a user has javascript enabled, headings should auto-expand and collapse as a user scrolls through the document, and the current entry is tracked twice over: its own text turns to the attention colour, and a [bounding box](#annotation) slides down the list to enclose it. Without javascript there is no current entry and so no box.

### Narrow viewports

The table of contents is in line with the content, after the abstract, in a collapsible section. Each level should also be collapsible. Collapse should be controlled by an indicator to the _left_ of that element. All are collapsed by default. This table of contents should _not_ auto-expand and collapse.

### Print

In print, the table of contents should be in line with the content, but always expanded.

## Headings

Hedings should be in a different font from the main content. They should be [anchor links](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/a) to themselves. On platforms with javascript enabled, an icon should appear on hover that copies the full anchor link to the clipboard. A top-level heading is underscored by a short brush stroke in ink, which is the same figure the front matter closes with, in the quieter colour.

## Links

Links in content should be semi-bold, but not a different color or underlined. Each carries a [provenance mark](#annotation) inside the anchor, so that the glyph travels with the link and is underlined along with it when hovered; it is hidden from screen readers, and from paper.

### Popover

Hovering over a link should show a popover, fixed to above the link, with a short preview. The popover can include any html content, but in general the first line should be the title, the second metadata in italics, and then the content. The popover should have a fixed maximum size, and can be scrolled if its contents are long. It reads as a pane of glass rather than as a slip of paper, in the idiom of a machine's overlay rather than a book's: a hard-edged rectangle, flat and uncoloured, clear enough that the page shows through it as a blur, with no border, no gradient and no hue of its own — the surfaces of the ink scheme lean faintly green, and glass does not. The pane stops a few pixels short of its [bounding box](#annotation) on every side, so the ticks stand clear at the corners with open page between them and the glass, and they are the only hard figure on it. Its title is in the display face. It rises the last few pixels into place as it fades in, and sinks as it fades out.

#### Default Style

By default, links should pull title, author, site/publication name, date and summary from [OpenGraph](https://ogp.me/) tags. Resolution should attempt to fall back to `<title>` and `<meta name="author">` tags if OpenGraph tags are not present.

#### Internal Pages

Internal pages should show the title, abstract, and site name, but not any other information.

#### DOIs

Links to https://doi.org that represent a valid Digital Object Identifier should pull title, author, date published, abstract and other metadata from the registrar that issued them: the [CrossRef DOI Api](https://api.crossref.org/swagger-ui/index.html) where it knows the identifier, and [content negotiation](https://citation.doi.org/docs.html) against doi.org otherwise, which is what reaches DOIs registered elsewhere. A registrar describes a work in its own vocabulary, so what it answers is translated into CSL-JSON before the site uses it; a DOI whose metadata cannot be translated is treated as unresolved rather than shown in part.

#### Wikipedia

Links to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia) should include the title, summary and infobox, as well as an image if one exists.

### Print

When printed, links should appear as regular text, since a printed link cannot be followed. A link with an entry in the [Bibliography](#bibliography) should be inline-cited in MLA format: e.g. (Author, Year). A link without one should print its full address in plaintext instead — that covers pages with no bibliography at all, and links to this site's own pages, which are never entries.

## Bibliography

If `bibliography: true` is set in the frontmatter, the article should include a "Works Referenced" section generated from every link in the article, in MLA style. Entries should be links to their source

#### Additional Citations

Articles can provide an additional list of citations under the `citation` frontmatter property. The list of citations should be in [bibjson](https://rufuspollock-okfn.github.io/bibjson/) format, validated by Astro's support for [frontmatter schema](https://rufuspollock-okfn.github.io/bibjson/).

## Sidenotes

When there is enough horizontal space and in print, footnotes should be displayed to the right of content, aligned with their footnote marker as much as possible. Out there they are marginal commentary: set in the lighter ink, held by a hairline on their left, and beginning outside the article's frame — and outside the [corner ticks](#annotation) that reach past it — rather than on top of either. Their markers and numbers are in the accent colour. Hovering over the footnote marker should visually highlight the associated sidenote, bringing it up to full ink and turning its rule to the accent colour. Sidenotes that are close together should not overlap, but instead make space for each other. [^demo]
On narrow viewports, sidenotes should be hidden by default. Clicking them should display the sidenote in line with the content, and clicking the sidenote or the sidenote marker should re-hide the sidenote.
This behavior should not require javascript.[^scripter]

[^demo]: So for example, this sidenote, which is much much much longer than it really needs to be for demonstration purposes, should not overlap with the sidenote below in any way. Instead, the following note is pushed down, so that the notes are completely readable, but still roughly on eye-level with their note locations.
