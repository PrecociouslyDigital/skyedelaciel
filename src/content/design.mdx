# Design Goals

Content on this site should be optimized for **reading prose on desktop**. Where possible, pages should **degrade gracefully** on other form factors (such as supporting vertical displays or touchscreens), support screen-readers with aria tags where appropriate, and **print well** directly from the browser. Elements should be padded pleasantly, both from each other and the edge of the screen.

Inspirations include Edward Tufte and Gwern.net.

The visual vocabulary is borrowed from Chinese calligraphy and the page furniture of a woodblock book. Paper and ink give the two screen schemes their names. A frame of a thick rule outside a thin one holds the text block. Notes sit outside that frame, as marginal commentary does. No Chinese characters are used as ornament; only the shapes are.

The colour comes from a herbarium: the pigments are those of flowering vines pressed in the New York Botanical Garden's, a wisteria violet and a leaf green. Rules between sections are vines rather than hairlines, after the vine-stem borders of illuminated manuscripts, painted in those two pigments.

A second hand has since read the page and marked it up. Surveillance interfaces share the grammar of scholarly apparatus — a box drawn around a subject, a readout in the margin, a timestamp that authenticates the record — and differ only in who drew them, so the machine's layer is added to the page rather than substituted for it. Where the scholar ruled a frame in ink, a machine sets corner ticks outside it in leaf green; where the byline authenticates the document by human authority, a revision stamp down the right rail authenticates it by build. Both are visible at once, and neither is allowed to set running text. See [Annotation](#annotation).

# Article Defaults

Most content on this site is a single article on a single page.

## Front Matter

An article's front matter (not to be conflated with markdown's `yaml` frontmatter) contains human-relevant metadata about the article. It should begin every article, visually set apart, closed off by a vine. Where the article itself is not framed — a narrow viewport, or paper — the front matter wears the frame instead, so a reader always meets one ruled block before the text.

### Title

The first front matter to appear should be the title, which should be visually more important than anything else in the article.

### Author

The author should appear after the title, less visually notable than most text. It should appear on the same line if the viewport has enough horizontal space, or wrap to its own line otherwise.

### Abstract

The abstract should follow the title and author. It should be formatted similarly to article text, but still stand out at a glance.

## Content

The main content of the article should have a maximum width of 80 characters. Where there is room on either side of it, the article is framed: a thick rule outside a thin one, with a channel of paper between them, and the text held off that frame by about two characters.

### Block elements

A blockquote hangs from a stroke of the [brush](#brushwork) down its left, in ink and ruled straight, and is never less than two lines tall, so that the stroke has room for its head and its tail. Bullets are marked in the accent colour and numbers in muted ink. Inline code sits on a faintly tinted ground; a code block sits on the raised surface inside a single hairline. A table rules its header row off with a heavy stroke of ink and each body row with a light one in the border colour, each row in a different stroke from the one above it, and sets its column headers in the same small caps as the rest of the apparatus. A horizontal rule is a vine, centred and well short of the measure.

### Brushwork

Every straight line that is not a rule is painted with the brush that painted the logo: the same model of a stroke, ported from the logo's own. It touches down with a lopsided head, wanders a little off its line, swells where it is pressed into a turn, thins as the ink runs low, and runs dry at its end into filaments of uneven length. Its edges have the tooth of paper, and soft bulges where the ink pooled. A stroke is drawn in the logo's units, 19.5 of them wide as the hexagon's strokes are, and scaled to wherever it is laid. A line that has to stay straight, such as a quotation's, is ruled: the hand wanders about a quarter as far off it and the edges are calmer.

A line's length is not known until the page is laid out, so its stroke is drawn once and cut into three: head, body and tail. The head and tail keep their shape at the ends of the line, each three times as long as the stroke's band is thick, and only the body stretches between them. Each piece is a mask rather than a picture, so a stroke takes whatever ink its element paints through it and needs no copy per scheme. A stroke is fixed by its seed, so the same line always shows the same stroke.

### Vines

Every rule on the site is a flowering vine, after the vine-stem borders of illuminated manuscripts: one pen-drawn stem running left to right in arcs of uneven size, now and then looping over itself, with shoots that leave it as a pen would and curl into open or tight spirals. Small ivy leaves sit along the stems at varied angles, a few roses close the larger curls, and hairline tendrils fill what room is left. A curl grows only from the main stem, never from another curl, and nothing grows inside a curl but its own rose. The stem and leaves are leaf green and the roses wisteria violet; leaves and roses are washed, a darker rim round a paler fill.

A vine set flush left grows one way, pressed down where the pen began and rolling up at its tip; one centred in the column tapers to both ends, fullest in the middle. It still reads as a single line at a glance: the vine is ornament on a rule, not an illustration. Each place a rule appears — the front matter, a top-level heading, a horizontal rule, the start of a closing section — always draws the same vine, at its own fixed size.

The [table of contents](#table-of-contents) is the one place a vine is drawn to a length rather than a size: each of its sections grows a vine down beside its entries, drawn for exactly as many entries as the section holds, so that it ends in its curl beside the last of them. Those vines bear no roses of their own, since every rose on them marks an entry.

## Colors

There should be three color schemes: paper (light), ink (dark) and print. Paper and ink should default to the system preference, based on the css media query `prefers-color-scheme`, but should also be toggleable by the user (see [Navbar](#navbar)). Once a user sets a theme preference, that preference should be persisted to local storage and reloaded as early as possible in page load. Print is a scheme of its own and follows no preference.

Ink is chalk on a blackboard: a cool, nearly neutral slate under dusty off-white, with its chromas the same pigments made light enough to read on it.

Every scheme names the same roles, and only those: a `background`, a `raised` surface for what sits on top of it, `text` and a quieter `muted` for apparatus and marginal notes, a `border` rule with a fainter `border-faint` for tinted ground, three chromas — `accent`, `signal` and `attention` — a `shadow`, and the `grain` of the article's texture. A scheme that omits a role, or invents one, should fail the build, as should one whose text falls below 7:1 against its own background where the grain lies thickest, or whose muted text or any of whose chromas falls below 4.5:1. Print is the boldest of the three schemes, and that too is a build error rather than a matter of taste.

Where the article is framed, the ground inside the frame is textured, and only there. On the board it is what an eraser leaves: broad sideways sweeps where it went, and a fine speckle of the dust it missed. On paper it is a sheet's own grain, a faint warm mottle under an even tooth. Print carries no texture; the sheet it is printed on has its own.

The three chromas are three hands, and none of them ever sets running text.

| Role        | Whose hand it is                                    | Where it appears                                                                                            |
| ----------- | --------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `accent`    | the author — stamped, rare, permanent               | sidenote numbers, bullets, the focus ring                                                                   |
| `signal`    | the machine that read the page — ambient, constant  | corner ticks, the rail slug, link provenance marks                                                          |
| `attention` | right now — hot, transient, gone when you look away | the current entry in the table of contents, the underline under a hovered link, a link that did not resolve |

The chromas are mixed from pressed vines in the [C. V. Starr Virtual Herbarium](https://sweetgum.nybg.org/science/vh/). `accent` is the violet of _Wisteria frutescens_ ([NY 02532657](http://sweetgum.nybg.org/science/vh/specimen_details.php?irn=2764131)); `attention` is the mauve of _Clitoria mariana_ ([NY 03106574](http://sweetgum.nybg.org/science/vh/specimen_details.php?irn=3355261)); `signal` is a forest green, chosen rather than sampled, since every leaf on the sheets has dried to olive. Only two hues, violet and green: `attention` is a second violet rather than a third hue because a link that did not resolve has to be told apart from one that did at a glance, and the two marks differ only in colour.

Each pigment keeps its hue and chroma, and each scheme sets its lightness at build time, just far enough from the ground to stand at 6.5:1 where the grain lies thickest — 9:1 in print — so no one chroma shouts louder than the others by contrast alone, and a palette cannot drift out of that band.

## Annotation

The machine layer is one idea, and everything drawn in `signal` belongs to it: a second reader has been over this page and left its working visible. It never writes words of its own — it draws boxes and it stamps identifiers, which is the whole of its vocabulary.

It draws a **bounding box** as four corner ticks, a right angle at each corner with the edges between them left open, around anything it has taken as a subject: the article, outside the ink frame rather than replacing it, so that both readers' boxes stand; a code block; the popover on a link; the entry of the [table of contents](#table-of-contents) being read. That last box never lands on the contents' vines: around a whole section — an entry with what it unfolded — it reaches out past the vine the section grows on, and around a single entry it hugs the words and stops short of the vine and its marks. Where the thing it has boxed can scroll, the ticks are painted on it rather than around it, since a box drawn outside a scrolling element would be clipped.

It draws a **rail slug** down the right margin of a framed article, set in a vertical column in the monospace face — the 書口 of a woodblock page, which carries the title and fascicle number down the fold. The column runs downward but the characters stand upright in it, as they do on a seal, rather than being turned on their side. It dates and counts the page: published, edited if it has been, and the revision the build was made from. An unedited page carries no edited date, and outside a git checkout the revision is simply absent. On [Tumblr](#tumblr) it stamps each post with its day and, once it has any, its count of notes.

It draws a **provenance mark** after every link in the prose: a single glyph naming what kind of address it is — `§` for a page of this site, `↗` for anywhere else, `¶` for a DOI (an arXiv paper included, since it has one), `W` for Wikipedia. The mark is a property of the kind, so a kind without one should fail to compile. Whether the link resolved is a separate question and is answered by colour rather than by a second glyph: a link nothing answered for keeps its own mark, drawn in `attention`. A same-document fragment is not unresolved — there was nothing to look up.

The boxes and the marks are not printed: paper carries no live annotation, and a tick drawn on a sheet is a mark no reader can follow up. The rail slug is the exception and prints with the page, standing in the clear strip the text block leaves beside it. It is a dateline rather than an affordance, and a sheet separated from its address has nothing else to say when it was written or which revision it is.

## Typography

Prose is set in Source Serif 4, the Latin companion of Source Han Serif and so the text face that sits comfortably beside Song-style type. Display type — an article's title and every heading — is set in Cormorant, whose high contrast and drawn-out terminals read as brush work at display sizes. Apparatus — navigation links, the byline, column headers, the contents toggle — is the prose face set as letterspaced small caps, so that labels stay in the same voice as the text while reading as captions. Code is set in whatever monospace the reader already has.

Both families are variable, and both are self-hosted: every font file a page loads should come from this site's own origin, so that reading a page announces the reader to nobody else.

The scale runs from the article title, which is the largest thing on the page, down through the six heading levels to body text at 1.0625rem on a line height of 1.6. Figures in prose are old-style, so that a number sits in the line of text rather than standing above it; figures in tables and in ordered-list markers are lining and tabular, so that columns of them align.

## Motion

A readout snaps to where it belongs rather than easing into it. Every transition on the site is linear, runs no longer than 120ms, and moves only geometry or colour; a longer one should fail the build rather than be caught by eye. Delays are exempt from that limit and are a separate tool — the link popover waits before it appears, so that a pointer crossing a link on its way somewhere else never summons one. A change tied to scrolling, like a popover's banner giving way to its text, is not a transition: it has no duration, and moves exactly as far as the reader scrolls.

What grows is the exception. It is not a readout but a plant, or a hand, and it moves at a brush's pace and with a brush's easing, in no more than 600ms: the logo when it is [rewritten](#content-1), and the [table of contents](#table-of-contents) as its sections unfold and fold away. A section grows open in about half a second and folds in less, its growing edge soft rather than cut; the shoot over its ring unrolls into its vine as it opens and curls back as it folds. Each entry arrives as the growing tip reaches it, and resolves: coarse blocks of its own ink, finer blocks, finer still, then its words through a moment's blur, as a picture loads at rising resolutions. Folding plays the same backwards, last entry first. The blocks are not drawn from the letters, only cut to about their size.

A reader who has asked for reduced motion gets none of it: under `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce` every transition duration is zero, and nothing grows or resolves — a section is simply open or shut. That answer should travel with each transition rather than being applied once over the top of everything, so a component that grows a new one cannot forget it. A change tied to scrolling is held still too, and the logo is never rewritten.

# Pages

Besides its articles, the site has a few pages of its own, set in the same shell and opened by the same [front matter](#front-matter). A list of pieces looks the same wherever it appears: each piece's title in the display face, the day it came out beside it as apparatus, and its abstract under them, newest first.

## Home

The home page's title is the site's name, and its abstract is a short introduction. Under them are the latest pieces from every section. It has no table of contents.

## Section pages

A section of the site is a directory of articles with an `index.mdx` of its own. That file is the section's page, at the directory's path, and every article under the directory belongs to the section. A section page is written like any other page: a title and an abstract, `##` headings to group its pieces, and links to them and to work published elsewhere, with all the usual [popovers](#popover). It has no dates of its own, and so no byline and no [stamp](#annotation).

What the page does not link to is not lost. It closes with **Everything Else**: the section's published pieces that the page does not link to, set off by a long vine as [works referenced](#bibliography) are, and listed in the table of contents beside the page's own groups. When the page links to every piece, the group and its entry are left out.

## Not found

An address with no page behind it is answered with a page that says so, in the same shell, with links home and to each section.

## Publication

An article is out from the day its `published` date names, in Los Angeles. Until then it is scheduled: it is built only under `/scheduled/`, where it reads exactly as it will once it is out, and where only the author can reach it. Cloudflare Access asks for a one-time PIN at that prefix, at every address the site is served from. Nothing else on the site knows a scheduled article is there: it is in no list of pieces, no feed, no sitemap and no popover, and its own address is not found. The site is built again every day just after midnight in Los Angeles, and the first build on an article's day moves it to its own address.

A published page may not link to a scheduled one, and a scheduled article may not link to one that comes out after it. Either fails the build, rather than shipping a link that leads nowhere on the day it goes out.

# Components

## Navbar

### Wide viewports

When there is enough space for both the navbar and sidenotes, the navbar sits to the side of the content, right-aligned to its left edge, and is arranged vertically. The column it sits in is as wide as the sidenotes' on the other side, so the article stands in the middle of the page.

### Narrow viewports

The navbar is horizontal, in line with the content, before the title. It should match the content's maximum width so they align visually. The seal, the links and the scheme toggle share one band, unboxed, and every link is at least a fingertip (44px) tall. The links sit in centred rows, more on top than below, the lower row set between the upper one's so that no tap zone sits straight under another: three links go two over one where they will not fit on a line, and five would go three over two. On the narrowest screens the band gives a little rather than wrapping again — the seal, the type and the space round each link shrink with the width of the page, down to what still reads. The toggle is drawn small but answers a fingertip all round it, and nothing in the band lets the page scroll sideways.

### Print

The Navbar should not be shown on print pages, and should make space so that the content is aligned to the left edge of the page.

### Content

The navbar contains, in order

- A clickable logo that takes you to the homepage
- Links to the site's landing pages — Fiction, Nonfiction and [Tumblr](#tumblr) — set as apparatus, a size larger than the byline. The one for where the reader is now is set in full ink rather than muted, and marked as the current page for a screen reader.
- A button that toggles the [Color Scheme](#colors)

The logo is drawn as a seal: the mark, a woven six-petal flower painted in brush strokes, inside a square border of the same ink, in the scheme's `text` colour. It is set into the page rather than served as an image, so that it reads in both schemes without a second copy of the artwork, and so that it can be rewritten: when the pointer comes onto it, its strokes lift off one after another in the direction they were painted and are laid down again. Once begun, the rewrite plays to its end even if the pointer leaves. The scheme toggle is a small square, matching the seal and the frame rather than the circle it used to be.

## Table of contents

### Wide viewports

When there is enough space for both the table of contents and sidenotes, the table of contents sits to the left of the content, arranged vertically, underneath the navbar. As the user scrolls, the top of the table of contents should "stick" to the top of the viewport (with pleasant margin, of course). When the contents are taller than the window leaves them, they scroll on their own while the navbar stays put, and their ends fade while there is more beyond them.

The contents are a trellis of [vines](#vines). Only the top level folds (so you'd expand a specific `h1` to see every `h2` and `h3` under it); the second level is always shown with its section, and lines up with the first. Collapse is controlled by an indicator to the _right_ of each top-level entry: an iron ring on a bolt from a square plate, with a shoot draped over it, hanging down the column beside the words. Folded, the shoot ends in a tight curl; as the section unfolds the curl unrolls downward and runs on into the section's own vine, which grows down that column beside the section's entries and ends in its curl beside the last of them. The vine is what groups a section's entries together. Each second-level entry is marked on it by a large ivy leaf with a rose set in the middle of its blade. The third level recedes, as an `h3` does in the article: set in from the second, in italic and a step smaller, and each of its entries is reached by a shoot of its own off the vine, which drops along the stem, turns out through a rounded elbow and runs toward the words, stopping a little short of them in a curl. How it all grows is described under [Motion](#motion).

When a user has javascript enabled, the entry for the section being read is current: its section unfolds, every other section folds, and it is tracked twice over — its own text turns to the attention colour, and a [bounding box](#annotation) slides down the list to enclose it together with everything it unfolded. Where the contents scroll on their own, they bring the current entry into view once its section has finished growing, near the top so that what it unfolded shows below it. The section being read is the one the top of the viewport is in, whichever way the reader scrolled there; over the last screenful of the page that line runs down to the bottom of the viewport, so that a short last section is reached too. Without javascript there is no current entry and so no box, but the sections still unfold and grow by hand.

### Narrow viewports

The table of contents is in line with the content, after the abstract, in a collapsible section that grows open and folds away as a section of it does. It is the same trellis, mirrored: the rings, the vines and the shoots grow on the _left_ of the words, and so does the collapse indicator. All are collapsed by default. This table of contents should _not_ auto-expand and collapse.

### Print

In print, the table of contents should be in line with the content, always expanded, and bare: no vines, no rings, nothing to unfold.

## Headings

Hedings should be in a different font from the main content. They should be [anchor links](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Reference/Elements/a) to themselves. On platforms with javascript enabled, an icon should appear on hover that copies the full anchor link to the clipboard. A top-level heading is underscored by a short vine.

## Links

Links in content should be semi-bold, but not a different color or underlined. Each carries a [provenance mark](#annotation) inside the anchor, so that the glyph travels with the link and is underlined along with it when hovered; it is hidden from screen readers, and from paper.

### Popover

Hovering over a link should show a popover, fixed to above the link, with a preview of the page it leads to: the title first, then a line of metadata in italics, then the summary. Where the source knows more, the facts follow the summary as a ledger. It comes after the preview rather than beside it, so a reader who wants the facts scrolls on to them. Only Wikipedia articles have one. The popover has a fixed width and a fixed maximum height, and its text scrolls if it is longer than that. It reads as a pane of glass rather than as a slip of paper, in the idiom of a machine's overlay rather than a book's: a hard-edged rectangle, flat and uncoloured, clear enough that the page shows through it as a blur, with no border, no gradient and no hue of its own — the surfaces of the ink scheme lean faintly green, and glass does not. The pane stops a few pixels short of its [bounding box](#annotation) on every side, so the ticks stand clear at the corners with open page between them and the glass. The ticks are the glass's only hard figure; the ledger's rules are the only other one, drawn on it as the site's tables are, ink over the first row and a hairline under each, with its labels set as apparatus. Its title is in the display face. It rises the last few pixels into place as it fades in, and sinks as it fades out.

A link's image is set into the glass, inset as far as the glass is from its ticks: down the right edge in a column if the image is tall, across the top as a banner if it is wide. Only the text scrolls. A column shows its image whole, never cropped or squeezed, and runs it from the top of the glass to the bottom: the pane takes the height of the image, and the image holds still beside the text as it scrolls. A banner crops its image to fill the pane's width; a pane with one is allowed to stand taller, and as the text scrolls up the banner gives way to it, giving up less than a third of its height. An image goes in whichever box suits its shape: a column that would be left mostly empty, or a banner that would crop most of the image away, is the wrong box. The two losses are the same ratio of the image's shape to the box's, so the shape at which both boxes suit an image equally, about 1.44 wide to 1 tall, follows from the boxes rather than being chosen. An image too small to fill its box without being enlarged is left out: it is an icon or a logo rather than a picture, and blown up it is only a blur. Images are copied when the site is built and served from this site, as its fonts are, so that opening a popover announces the reader to nobody else; and an image is fetched only when its popover opens.

#### Default Style

By default, links should pull title, author, site/publication name, date and summary from [OpenGraph](https://ogp.me/) tags. Resolution should attempt to fall back to `<title>` and `<meta name="author">` tags if OpenGraph tags are not present. An OpenGraph image that would tell the reader nothing is dropped: a card generated by a host such as GitHub, which only sets the page's title in type, and a site's logo, recognised as the same image the site's homepage gives.

#### Internal Pages

Internal pages should show the title, abstract, and site name, but not any other information.

#### DOIs

Links to https://doi.org that represent a valid Digital Object Identifier should pull title, author, date published, abstract and other metadata from the registrar that issued them: the [CrossRef DOI Api](https://api.crossref.org/swagger-ui/index.html) where it knows the identifier, and [content negotiation](https://citation.doi.org/docs.html) against doi.org otherwise, which is what reaches DOIs registered elsewhere. A registrar describes a work in its own vocabulary, so what it answers is translated into CSL-JSON before the site uses it; a DOI whose metadata cannot be translated is treated as unresolved rather than shown in part.

A link to an [arXiv](https://arxiv.org) abstract or PDF is a DOI link too. arXiv registers every paper with DataCite under `10.48550/arXiv.` and its identifier, so the link resolves through that DOI, whichever version of the paper it names.

#### Wikipedia

Links to [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia) should include the title, the article's short description as the metadata line, the summary and the article's image if it has one. Its infobox follows as the ledger, capped at six rows; an article whose infobox cannot be read is shown without one.

### Print

When printed, links should appear as regular text, since a printed link cannot be followed. A link with an entry in the [Bibliography](#bibliography) should be inline-cited in MLA format: e.g. (Author, Year). A link without one should print its full address in plaintext instead — that covers pages with no bibliography at all, and links to this site's own pages, which are never entries.

## Bibliography

If `bibliography: true` is set in the frontmatter, the article should include a "Works Referenced" section generated from every link in the article, in MLA style. Entries should be links to their source

#### Additional Citations

Articles can provide an additional list of citations under the `citation` frontmatter property. The list of citations should be in [bibjson](https://rufuspollock-okfn.github.io/bibjson/) format, validated by Astro's support for [frontmatter schema](https://rufuspollock-okfn.github.io/bibjson/).

## Sidenotes

When there is enough horizontal space and in print, footnotes should be displayed to the right of content, aligned with their footnote marker as much as possible. Out there they are marginal commentary: set in the lighter ink, held by a light [stroke](#brushwork) down their left, and beginning outside the article's frame — and outside the [corner ticks](#annotation) that reach past it — rather than on top of either. Their markers and numbers are in the accent colour. Hovering over the footnote marker should visually highlight the associated sidenote, bringing it up to full ink and turning its stroke to the accent colour. Sidenotes that are close together should not overlap, but instead make space for each other. [^demo]
On narrow viewports, sidenotes should be hidden by default. Clicking them should display the sidenote in line with the content, and clicking the sidenote or the sidenote marker should re-hide the sidenote.
This behavior should not require javascript.[^scripter]

[^demo]: So for example, this sidenote, which is much much much longer than it really needs to be for demonstration purposes, should not overlap with the sidenote below in any way. Instead, the following note is pushed down, so that the notes are completely readable, but still roughly on eye-level with their note locations.

[^scripter]: Thanks to [A Scripter's notes](https://scripter.co/sidenotes-using-only-css/) for sharing a bunch of CSS tricks that make this possible.

## Tumblr

The blog at [skyedelaciel.tumblr.com](https://skyedelaciel.tumblr.com) is another part of the site rather than a site of its own: it is set in the same shell, with the same navbar, faces and schemes, and the navbar marks Tumblr as where the reader is. Its theme is built out of this site's own layout and stylesheet, so that the two cannot drift apart. Only the blog's own address wears it; Tumblr's views of the blog inside its dashboard and app do not use themes.

An index page opens with the blog's title and description as its front matter. Each post is an article of its own, framed and stamped as an article of the site is, with its title, if it has one, as front matter, and the blog it was reblogged from as its author. A reblog sets out each hand's addition in turn, each under its own name. Pages of posts are paged rather than scrolled endlessly, so that a page is a bounded document like any other, with links to older and newer pages at its foot.

The table of contents carries tags. On an index page it lists the posts, each named by its day and its title, with its tags folded under it, and follows the reader as the contents of an article do: the post being read unfolds its tags. A post's own page lists that post's tags. Where there is no sidebar, a post's tags close it instead. A post's own page closes with its notes, set off from it as [works referenced](#bibliography) are from an article.
